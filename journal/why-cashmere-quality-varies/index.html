Why Cashmere Quality Varies So Much (And How to Tell the Difference)

Cashmere is not a quality standard. It is the name of a fibre, and the distance between the best and worst versions of it is wider than in almost any other material.

By Dr. Connor Robertson, Founder of Seymour Maison

There is no other material in the luxury wardrobe where the word on the label and the object in your hands can diverge so completely. Two sweaters, both one hundred percent cashmere, both honestly labelled, can differ in cost by a factor of eight and in service life by a factor of twenty. Neither label is lying. The word simply covers too much ground.

Cashmere is the fine undercoat of a goat, most of it raised across the high plateau of Inner Mongolia, Mongolia, and parts of northern China and Iran. A single animal produces somewhere between one hundred and two hundred grams of usable fibre per year. A good sweater takes the annual yield of three to five goats. That constraint is real, it is not marketing, and it is the reason genuine cashmere cannot be cheap.

What has changed is not the supply of goats. It is the industry's skill at producing something technically compliant with the label from raw material that would once have been rejected.

Fibre Length Decides Almost Everything

If you remember one variable, make it staple length. Cashmere combed by hand from the goat's undercoat in spring produces long fibres, typically thirty-four millimetres and above. Fibre shorn mechanically and separated afterwards produces a mix that includes a great deal of short material, often under thirty millimetres.

Long fibres can be spun into yarn with fewer ends exposed at the surface. Fewer exposed ends means less migration, less tangling, less pilling, and a garment that holds together under abrasion. Short fibres produce yarn that feels identical in the shop and disintegrates in service. The two are indistinguishable to the hand on day one. By week six they are obviously different objects.

This is the fundamental honesty problem in the cashmere trade. The quality difference is real, substantial, and invisible at exactly the moment the customer makes the decision.

Micron Count and the Softness Trap

Micron count measures fibre diameter. Fourteen to fifteen and a half microns is genuinely fine. Sixteen to seventeen is good commercial grade. Above eighteen, the fibre starts to feel closer to fine lambswool than to what most people expect from cashmere.

Finer is generally better, but finer is not the same as softer, and this is where most buying decisions go wrong. Softness in a shop can be manufactured. Aggressive brushing, chemical softening, and heavy washing all produce a surface that feels extraordinary on the shelf. All three work by disturbing the fibre surface, and all three shorten the life of the garment.

The reliable signal is not maximum softness. It is softness combined with recovery. Press your hand into the fabric and lift it. Cashmere with structural integrity springs back. Cashmere that has been beaten into softness holds the impression.

Ply, Gauge, and Weight

Ply describes how many strands are twisted together into the knitting yarn. Two ply is the sensible default for a sweater intended to last. Single ply produces lightweight garments that feel luxurious and wear thin at the elbows within two seasons. Higher plies, four, six, or more, build substantial winter knitwear with real structure.

Gauge describes how tightly the yarn is knitted. A twelve gauge two ply sweater and a seven gauge two ply sweater are entirely different garments despite sharing a ply count. Weight, given in grams, cuts through both numbers and is the most useful single figure a maker can disclose. A serious cashmere crewneck for winter sits somewhere between four hundred and seven hundred grams. Anything substantially under three hundred is a transitional piece, whatever the price suggests.

What to Do in Practice

Buy from makers who publish the specification. Micron, ply, gauge, weight, and origin of fibre. A house that knows these numbers and states them plainly has a supply chain it can see. A house that answers with adjectives has a supply chain that stops at an agent.

Accept a small amount of early pilling and deal with it properly. A cashmere comb, used gently after the first few wears, removes surface migration and the garment then stabilises. Do not use a battery powered fabric shaver on good knitwear, which cuts rather than lifts and thins the surface permanently.

Wash it yourself in cool water with a wool detergent, and dry it flat. Dry cleaning cashmere repeatedly is worse for the fibre than hand washing, a fact that surprises most owners and costs them a great deal of money over a lifetime.

Above all, apply the same test you would apply to any material claim. Ask what the maker actually knows about the fibre in front of you. The answer, or the absence of one, tells you more than the price. It is the same principle we set out in Good Cotton, Great Cotton, and it holds across every fibre in the wardrobe.

Common Questions

How can you tell good cashmere from bad cashmere in a shop?

Three checks take about thirty seconds. Stretch a section gently and release it: good cashmere returns to shape, poor cashmere stays slack. Rub the surface firmly with your palm for several seconds and look for immediate pilling, which indicates short fibres that were brushed to fake loft. Hold the garment up to light and look at the knit density: if light passes through freely at a mid gauge, the yarn is thin and the sweater will thin further. Excessive softness on the shelf is a warning rather than a virtue.

What do micron count and ply actually mean?

Micron count measures the diameter of an individual fibre. Fine cashmere sits between fourteen and sixteen microns, and lower numbers feel softer against skin. Ply counts how many yarn strands are twisted together to form the knitting yarn. Two ply is standard for a durable sweater, single ply is lighter and more fragile, and heavier plies build warmth and structure. Ply and gauge together matter more than ply alone, since a two ply yarn knitted loosely can still produce a thin garment.

Why does some cashmere pill so quickly?

Pilling is caused by short fibres migrating to the surface and tangling. Long fibre cashmere, combed from the undercoat rather than shorn and separated mechanically, has fewer loose ends and pills far less. Heavy surface brushing during finishing produces an immediate softness that customers reward at the point of sale, but it drags short fibres to the surface and guarantees pilling within weeks. Some early pilling is normal in any cashmere. Continuous pilling after the first season is a fibre quality problem.
