Sustainability in Luxury Fashion: Beyond the Marketing

Almost every sustainability claim in fashion describes how a garment was made. Almost none describe how long it will last, which is the only figure that changes the outcome.

By Dr. Connor Robertson, Founder of Seymour Maison

Sustainability in fashion has developed a vocabulary problem. The words available, organic, recycled, responsible, conscious, all describe inputs. They describe what went into a garment at the moment of manufacture. None of them describe what happens afterward, and afterward is where nearly all of the environmental consequence lives.

This is not an accident of language. Inputs are measurable, certifiable, and auditable at a single point in time. Outcomes are none of those things. So the industry measures what it can measure and reports it as though it were the answer.

The Denominator Everyone Ignores

Take any environmental figure for a garment: litres of water, kilograms of carbon, hectares of land. Whatever the number, it is a numerator. To convert it into something meaningful you must divide by the number of times the garment is worn.

A t-shirt with a production footprint of twenty units, worn two hundred times, costs one tenth of a unit per wear. The same shirt worn ten times before losing shape costs two units per wear, twenty times worse. The material inputs are identical in both cases. Every certification on the label is identical. The environmental outcome differs by a factor of twenty.

Nothing in the current certification landscape captures this, because durability cannot be verified at the factory gate. It can only be verified by the passage of time, which no auditor is present for.

Where Organic Cotton Actually Helps and Where It Does Not

Organic cotton is worth defending on its merits, which are real and specific. Removing synthetic pesticides from cultivation improves soil health and reduces contamination of local water systems, and the benefit to the people doing the farming is substantial and immediate.

What organic certification does not address is almost everything else. It says nothing about staple length, which determines whether yarn resists pilling. Nothing about spinning method. Nothing about dye chemistry or effluent treatment, which is where the water contamination in textiles overwhelmingly occurs. Nothing about the cutting room, the seam construction, or whether the garment survives fifty washes.

An organic cotton t-shirt made from short staple fibre with a cut collar and single needle seams is a worse environmental outcome than a conventionally grown one built to last a decade. The label is honest. The implication is not.

The Recycled Polyester Trap

Recycled polyester is the clearest case of a partial solution marketed as a total one. Diverting bottles from waste is genuinely useful. The material still sheds microfibres in every wash, at rates comparable to virgin polyester, and those fibres pass through treatment systems into waterways.

Worse, the recycling is terminal. A bottle becomes a garment and the garment becomes nothing. It is a one way transfer that consumes a recyclable material and produces a non recyclable one, described in the language of circularity.

Natural fibres have their own significant costs, and cotton in particular is water intensive. But a cotton garment at end of life can biodegrade or be mechanically reclaimed. That distinction matters over decades, and the marketing on both sides tends to select whichever timeframe flatters the story.

What an Honest Position Looks Like

We take a narrow view, because a narrow view is the only one we can actually defend. We do not claim to be a sustainable company. We claim to make a small number of garments intended to last a very long time, and we believe the second thing does more good than the first thing would if we said it.

That means limited production runs rather than seasonal volume. It means fabric chosen for service life first, which we describe in our piece on fabric weight. It means construction decisions, knitted collars, reinforced shoulder seams, that add cost without adding anything visible at the point of sale. And it means telling people how to care for a garment properly, because laundering is where most clothing dies.

We have written the fuller version of this argument in Longevity as Sustainability. The short version is that the most environmentally significant thing about a garment is how many mornings it gets reached for, and that is decided in the factory long before it is decided in anyone's conscience.

Questions Worth Asking

Ask a maker how long they expect a garment to last, and whether they will repair it. The answer, or the discomfort it produces, is more informative than any certificate.

Ask how many units were produced. Scale is the variable that no amount of input substitution corrects.

Ask what happens to unsold stock. Destruction of excess inventory remains widespread, and it is the point where the gap between stated values and operating reality is widest.

The answers will not fit on a hangtag. That is rather the point.

Common Questions

Is organic cotton actually better for the environment?

It is better on some axes and worse on others, which is why the blanket claim is misleading. Organic cultivation avoids synthetic pesticides and fertilisers, which benefits soil and local water systems. It also yields less per hectare, so producing the same quantity requires more land and often more water. Organic certification says nothing about spinning quality, dyeing chemistry, factory conditions, or how long the finished garment survives, and those account for most of the difference in real impact.

Does recycled polyester solve the microplastic problem?

No. Recycled polyester diverts material from waste streams, usually plastic bottles, which is a genuine benefit at the input stage. It sheds microfibres during washing at essentially the same rate as virgin polyester, and once it becomes a textile it typically cannot be recycled again. It is an improvement on one dimension that leaves the fundamental issue untouched, and it is frequently marketed as though it resolved the entire question.

What is the single most effective thing a buyer can do?

Wear each garment more times. Impact per wear is total production and disposal impact divided by the number of wears, and no material substitution available today moves that number as far as doubling the wear count. A garment worn two hundred times has roughly a tenth the per wear footprint of the same garment worn twenty times. This is unglamorous, it cannot be certified, and it is worth more than every input claim combined.
