The Provenance Problem: On Fabric Sourcing and What Origin Actually Guarantees

A country of origin is not a standard. It is a postcode. The distance between the two accounts for most of what separates a fine garment from a merely expensive one.

Fabric sourcing is the least photogenic part of making clothes and the part that determines almost everything. It happens in correspondence, in sample requests, in the slow accumulation of small disappointments. No one romanticises it, and there is a reason: it consists largely of saying no.

The industry has trained the buyer to read origin as a proxy for quality. Egyptian cotton. Japanese mill. Made in Italy. These phrases have been repeated so often, and applied so loosely, that they now function as decoration rather than information. Egypt grows extraordinary extra-long staple cotton and it also grows a great deal of ordinary cotton. Italy houses some of the finest finishing houses in the world and a considerable number of unremarkable ones. The label tells you where. It does not tell you which.

What Is Actually Being Chosen

A fabric is the outcome of at least four separate decisions, and each one can be made well or made cheaply.

The first is the fibre itself: staple length, uniformity, the conditions in which it was grown and how it was harvested. Longer fibres produce yarn with fewer exposed ends, which is why they resist pilling and why they feel smooth rather than merely soft. Softness can be added later, temporarily, with chemistry. Fibre length cannot be added at all.

The second is the spinning. Compact and ring-spun yarns behave differently under tension, take dye differently, and age differently. A mill that spins slowly, with tighter tolerances, produces yarn of noticeably greater consistency, and it charges accordingly.

The third is the knitting. Gauge, tension, and the patience of the machine determine whether a jersey has body or merely weight. Two fabrics can share an identical composition and gram weight and feel entirely unrelated in the hand.

The fourth is the finishing: the washing, the setting, the treatments applied at the end. This is where a great deal of deception occurs. A heavily finished fabric performs beautifully in a showroom and surrenders its character in three washes. A properly constructed one gives less at first, and then holds.

The Cost of Asking

Requesting this information is straightforward. Receiving it is not. Many suppliers cannot answer questions about their own supply chain beyond the immediate step, not from evasion but because the chain has been abstracted into a series of intermediaries, each of whom knows only their own link. A mill may not know the gin. The gin may not know the farm.

Sourcing responsibly means declining to work at that level of abstraction. It means fewer options, longer lead times, and higher unit costs. It also means the ability to state, without hedging, what a garment is made of and where each stage occurred. That claim is either earned or it is marketing.

Why It Rarely Happens

The economics discourage it. A brand producing at volume needs fabric availability above all else, and availability favours the large, generic supplier who can deliver ten thousand metres on short notice. Specificity is the enemy of scale. The moment a house commits to a particular spinner and a particular finish, it accepts a ceiling on how quickly it can grow.

We accepted that ceiling deliberately. It is the reason our production runs are small and the reason the list moves at the pace it does. A fabric worth using is not always available, and we would rather wait than substitute.

The Only Honest Test

None of this is verifiable by touch in a fitting room. Every fabric feels good new. The test is time: thirty washes, two summers, the collar after a year. A well-sourced jersey settles into itself and stays. A poorly sourced one reveals what was borrowed from the finishing rather than built into the cloth.

This is the uncomfortable truth about provenance. It is not a claim made at the point of sale. It is a claim the garment makes on its own behalf, slowly, long after the transaction is forgotten. Everything upstream exists to make certain that claim holds.
